\section{Transport phenomena in ideal gases}
	A gas molecule has finite mass and random thermal motion. Hence it possesses momentum as well as kinetic energy. Therefore, when a gas moves across different parts of a container, it can transport matter, energy or momentum.
	
	In an equilibrium state, there is no net transfer of matter, energy or momentum. However, if, in a non-equilibrium state, there is macroscopic motion of the gas in a particular direction, the following three cases may occur:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \emph{Different parts of the gas move with different velocities.}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Leads to relative motion between different layers of the gas.
			\item Hence, molecules crossing from faster moving layers transport greater momentum than those crossing from slower moving layers.
			\item The net transport of momentum in a given direction of motion across an imaginary plane is characterized by the \emph{coefficient of viscosity} $\eta$.
		\end{itemize}
		
		\item \emph{Different parts of the gas are at different temperatures.}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Molecules moving from regions of higher temperature to regions of lower temperature will carry greater thermal energy.
			\item This gives rise to thermal conduction, which is characterized by the thermal conductivity $K$.
		\end{itemize}
		
		\item \emph{Different parts of the gas have different concentrations.}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item Molecules migrating from regions of higher concentration to regions of lower concentration carry matter.
			\item This gives rise to diffusion, which is characterized by the coefficient of diffusion $D$.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\subsection{Viscosity: Transport of momentum}
		\setcounter{equation}{0}
		The property of a fluid by virtue of which it opposes the relative motion between adjacent layers is known as \emph{viscosity}. The \emph{coefficient of viscosity} $\eta$ is defined as the tangential force per unit area when a unit velocity gradient exists in a direction perpendicular to the direction of motion.
		\begin{figure}[h!]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/fig_viscosity-01}
		\end{figure}
		
		Consider two layers of the fluid which are separated by a distance $\dd{y}$, and moving with speeds $v$ and $v+\dd{v}$. Then the velocity gradient between these layers is $\dv{v}{y}$. If $F$ is the viscous force acting on an arbitrary area $A$, then we have
		\begin{align}
			F=-\eta A\dv{v}{y} \label{eqn:viscous-force}
		\end{align}
		
		Suppose a fluid has a velocity gradient of $\dv{v}{y}$ across its layers along the $y$-axis.
		\begin{figure}[h!]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/fig_viscosity-02}
		\end{figure}
		
		Then, if a molecule lying on the plane $xx'$ has a speed $u$, then a molecule at a height $\delta$ above the $xx'$ plane would have a speed of $u+\delta\dv{v}{y}$. Likewise, a molecule at a depth $\delta$ below $xx'$ would have a speed of $u-\delta\dv{v}{y}$.
		
		\begin{figure}[h!]
			\centering
			\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/fig_viscosity-03}
		\end{figure}
		Consider an infinitessimal volume $\dd{V}$ at a distance $r$ from an infinitessimal area $\dd{A}$ which lies on the $xx'$ plane as shown above.
		
		If $P_c$ is the collision frequency of the gas, then the average number of collisions per molecule in time $\dd{t}$ is $P_c\dd{t}$.
		
		For $n$ being the number density of the gas, the number of molecules contained within the volume $\dd{V}$ is $n\dd{V}$.
		
		Hence we have
		\begin{align*}
			\qty{\begin{matrix}\text{Total count of}\\ \text{collisions occurring}\\ \text{within $\dd{V}$ in time $\dd{t}$}\end{matrix}}&=\qty{\begin{matrix}\text{Collisions per}\\ \text{molecule}\\ \text{in time $\dd{t}$}\end{matrix}}\times\qty{\begin{matrix}\text{Number of molecules}\\ \text{contained within $\dd{V}$}\end{matrix}} \\
				&=\qty(P_c\dd{t})\times\qty(n\dd{V})=P_cn\dd{V}\dd{t}
		\end{align*}
		
		Because each collision involves two molecules, the total number of collisions taking place within $\dd{V}$ in time $\dd{t}$ is $\dfrac{1}{2}P_cn\dd{V}\dd{t}$.
		
		Now, each collision produces two free paths. Hence the number of free paths generated within the volume $\dd{V}$ in time $\dd{t}$ is $2\times\dfrac{1}{2}P_cn\dd{V}\dd{t}=P_cn\dd{V}\dd{t}$.
		
		The number of free paths, i.e. molecules, that are heading towards the area $\dd{A}$ from the volume $\dd{V}$ in time $\dd{t}$ is simply the number of free paths confined within the solid angle subtended by $\dd{A}$ at $\dd{V}$:
		\begin{align*}
			\dd{n}&=\dfrac{\dd{\Omega}}{4\pi}P_cn\dd{V}\dd{t}
		\end{align*}
		Also, the solid angle subtended by the area $\dd{A}$ at the volume $\dd{V}$ is given by
		\begin{align*}
			\dd{\Omega}&=\dfrac{\dd{A}\cos{\theta}}{r^2}
		\end{align*}
		where $\theta$ is the angle between $r$ and the $y$ axis. So, this gives us
		\begin{align*}
			\dd{n}&=P_cn\dfrac{\dd{A}\cos{\theta}}{4\pi r^2}\dd{V}\dd{t}
		\end{align*}
		
		Hence, the number of molecules that will reach $\dd{A}$ from $\dd{V}$ is given by the survival equation to be
		\begin{align*}
			\dd{N}&=\dd{n}\exp\qty(-\dfrac{r}{\lambda}) \\
				&=\qty(P_cn\dfrac{\dd{A}\cos{\theta}}{4\pi r^2}\dd{V}\dd{t})\exp\qty(-\dfrac{r}{\lambda}) \\
			\implies\dd{N}&=\dfrac{P_cn\cos{\theta}}{4\pi r^2}\dd{A}\dd{V}\dd{t}\exp\qty(-\dfrac{r}{\lambda})
		\end{align*}
		In spherical polar coordinates, the volume element becomes
		\begin{align*}
			\dd{V}=2\pi r^2\sin{\theta}\dd{\theta}\dd{r}
		\end{align*}
		Hence, we have
		\begin{align*}
			\dd{N}&=\dfrac{P_cn\cos{\theta}}{\cancelto{2}{4\pi r^2}}\dd{A}\qty(\cancel{2\pi r^2}\sin{\theta}\dd{\theta}\dd{r})\dd{t}\exp\qty(-\dfrac{r}{\lambda}) \\
				&=\dfrac{1}{2}P_cn \dd{A}\dd{t} \sin{\theta}\cos{\theta} \exp\qty(-\dfrac{r}{\lambda}) \dd{\theta}\dd{r}
		\end{align*}
		
		To calculate the total number of molecules crossing $\dd{A}$ in time $\dd{t}$ from the upper half-space above $xx'$, we integrate the above expression over $r$ (from $0$ to $\infty$) and over $\theta$ (from $0$ to $\pi/2$) as follows
		\begin{align*}
			N&=\dfrac{1}{2}P_cn \dd{A}\dd{t} \underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}{\exp\qty(-\dfrac{r}{\lambda})\dd{r}}}_{=\lambda} \underbrace{\int_{0}^{\pi/2}{\sin{\theta}\cos{\theta}\dd{\theta}}}_{=1/2} \\
			\implies N&=\dfrac{\lambda}{4}P_cn\dd{A}\dd{t} \\
				&=\dfrac{\cancel{\lambda}}{4} \dfrac{\expval{v}}{\cancel{\lambda}} n\dd{A}\dd{t} \\
			\implies\dfrac{N}{\dd{A}\dd{t}}&=\dfrac{1}{4}n\expval{v}
		\end{align*}
		
		The above expression gives us the number of molecules reaching the $xx'$ plane per unit area per unit time.
		
		We now calculate the total momentum transported by molecules above the $xx'$ plane after making its last collision at a height of $h$.
		
		\begin{figure}[h!]
			\centering
			\includegraphics*[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/fig_viscosity-h-above.png}
		\end{figure}
		
		\subsubsection{Zeroth order approximation}
			In this approximation, the height of last collision above $xx'$ is assumed to be one mean free path, i.e. $h=\lambda$. Then the speed of a molecule after its last collision is
			\begin{align*}
				v_+&=u+h\dv{v}{y} \\
				\implies v_+&=u+\lambda\dv{v}{y}
			\end{align*}
			where $u$ is the speed of the molecule at the $xx'$ plane.
			
			Hence, the momentum transported to the $xx'$ plane from above by a single molecule is
			\begin{align*}
				\Delta p_+&=mu-mv_+ \\
					&=mu-m\qty[u+\lambda\dv{v}{y}] \\
					&=\cancel{mu}-\cancel{mu}-m\lambda\dv{v}{y} \\
				\implies\Delta p_+&=-m\lambda\dv{v}{y}
			\end{align*}
			Then the net momentum transported to the $xx'$ plane per unit area per unit time by molecules from above is
			\begin{align*}
				P_+&=\qty(\dfrac{N}{\dd{A}\dd{t}})\times\qty(\Delta p_+) \\
					&=\qty(\dfrac{1}{4}n\expval{v})\times\qty(-m\lambda\dv{v}{y}) \\
				\implies P_+&=-\dfrac{1}{4}mn\expval{v}\lambda\dv{v}{y}
			\end{align*}
			
			\begin{figure}[h!]
				\centering
				\includegraphics*[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/fig_viscosity-h-below.png}
			\end{figure}
			
			Likewise, for molecules below the $xx'$ plane at a depth $h=\lambda$, the speed is
			\begin{align*}
				v_-&=u-\lambda\dv{v}{y}
			\end{align*}
			The momentum transported by a single molecule from below is
			\begin{align*}
				\Delta p_-&=mu-mv_- \\
				&=mu-m\qty[u-\lambda\dv{v}{y}] \\
				&=\cancel{mu}-\cancel{mu}+m\lambda\dv{v}{y} \\
				\implies\Delta p_-&=m\lambda\dv{v}{y}
			\end{align*}
			
			This gives the net momentum transported to the $xx'$ plane per unit area per unit time by molecules from below to be
			\begin{align*}
				P_-&=\dfrac{1}{4}mn\expval{v}\lambda\dv{v}{y}
			\end{align*}
			
			Therefore, the net momentum transported per unit area per unit time, across the $xx'$ plane is
			\begin{align}
				P&=P_+-P_- \nonumber\\
					&=\qty(-\dfrac{1}{4}mn\expval{v}\lambda\dv{v}{y})-\qty(\dfrac{1}{4}mn\expval{v}\lambda\dv{v}{y}) \nonumber\\
				\implies P&=-\dfrac{1}{2}mn\expval{v}\lambda\dv{v}{y} \label{eqn:mom-perarea-pertime}
			\end{align}
			
			This manifests as the viscous force, which is the momentum transported per unit time. Hence we must have
			\begin{align}
				P&=\dfrac{F}{A} \nonumber\\
				\implies P&=-\eta\dv{v}{y}\qq{[using eqn. (\ref{eqn:viscous-force})]} \label{eqn:P-alternate}
			\end{align}
			
			Comparing equations (\ref{eqn:mom-perarea-pertime}) and (\ref{eqn:P-alternate}), we obtain the zeroth order approximation of the coefficient of viscosity to be
			\begin{align*}
				\eta_0&=\dfrac{1}{2}mn\expval{v}\lambda
			\end{align*}
			
			Using the Clausius mean free path, i.e. $\lambda=\lambda_\text{Cl}=\dfrac{3}{4n\sigma}$, we get
			\begin{align*}
				\eta_0&=\dfrac{1}{2}m\cancel{n}\expval{v}\dfrac{3}{4\cancel{n}\sigma} \\
				\implies\Aboxed{\eta_0&=\dfrac{3m}{8\sigma}\expval{v}}
			\end{align*}
			
		\subsubsection{First order approximation}
			In this approximation, the exact average height of the last collision above $xx'$ is computed and found to be
			\begin{align*}
				h=\dfrac{2}{3}\lambda
			\end{align*}
			
			Using this value of $h$ and proceeding in the same manner as previously, we obtain the first order approximation of the coefficient of viscosity to be
			\begin{align}
				\eta_1&=\dfrac{1}{3}mn\expval{v}\lambda \label{eqn:coeff-visc}
			\end{align}
			
			Using the Clausius mean free path, i.e. $\lambda=\lambda_\text{Cl}=\dfrac{3}{4n\sigma}$ in equation (\ref{eqn:coeff-visc}), we get
			\begin{align*}
				\Aboxed{\eta_1&=\dfrac{m}{4\sigma}\expval{v}}
			\end{align*}
			
			Finally, using the Maxwell mean free path, i.e. $\lambda=\lambda_\text{M}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}n\sigma}$ in equation (\ref{eqn:coeff-visc}), we get
			\begin{align*}
				\Aboxed{\eta_M&=\dfrac{m}{3\sqrt{2}\sigma}\expval{v}}
			\end{align*}
			
			Because the collision cross-section is $\sigma=\pi d^2$, then the above equation can be written as
			\begin{align*}
				\eta_M&=\dfrac{m\expval{v}}{3\sqrt{2}\pi d^2} \\
				\implies  \Aboxed{d&=\sqrt{\dfrac{m\expval{v}}{3\sqrt{2}\pi \eta_M}}}
			\end{align*}
			In the above equation, the quantities $m$, $\expval{v}$ and $\eta_M$ are measurable. Therefore, this allows us to use these measurements to estimate the \emph{molecular diameter}, which is a microscopic property.
